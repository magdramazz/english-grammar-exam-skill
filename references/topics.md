## Present simple vs present continuous

**Test:** habit and permanent truth against action-happening-now and temporary arrangement; third-person `-s`; stative verbs that resist the continuous (know, believe, belong, seem, understand).

**Distractors:** the bare infinitive where `-s` is needed, the continuous where a stative verb blocks it, `is/are + base verb`, and the past form to punish a student skimming past `every day` / `at the moment`.

**Student errors:** dropping the third-person `-s`; `He is knowing the answer`; using the continuous for a habit because an adverb of frequency appears.

## Past simple vs past continuous

**Test:** the interrupted-action pattern (`while` + continuous, `when` + simple), background description, irregular past forms.

**Distractors:** the two tenses swapped across the clause boundary, `was/were` + base verb, the past participle standing in for the past simple (`had went`, `I have saw`).

**Student errors:** `didn't went`; using the simple for the long background action; wrong irregular participles.

---

## Present perfect

**Test:** unfinished time and present relevance against a finished past time; `since` vs `for`; `already / yet / just / ever / never`; the contrast where a past time expression makes the present perfect impossible.

**Distractors:** the past simple as the near-miss (this is the whole point of the topic at medium and hard level), `have` with a third-person subject, the base verb where a participle is needed, the continuous where a state verb blocks it.

**Student errors:** `I have seen him yesterday`; `since three years`; `has went`.

**Two traps when writing these items.** First, `have + participle` is naturally longer than `has + participle`, `did + base` or a bare past form, so with `I / you / we / they` subjects the correct option ends up the longest one and students guess by length. Lean on singular subjects, or give a distractor real length (`have been travelling`, `haven't been calling`). Second, "the continuous where a state verb blocks it" is only a distractor when no `since` / `for` is present — `have been living here since 2015` is correct English, and using it as a wrong option in such a sentence creates an item with two right answers that no checker can catch.

## Past perfect

**Test:** which of two past events came first, especially with `by the time`, `before`, `after`, `when`; the past perfect inside reported speech.

**Distractors:** the past simple (correct-sounding but loses the sequence), `had` + base verb, the present perfect, and the two clauses' tenses swapped.

**Student errors:** using the past simple in both clauses; `had wrote`.

---

## Future forms

**Test:** `will` for a decision made now or a prediction, `going to` for intention and visible evidence, the present continuous for a fixed arrangement, and the present simple in time clauses after `when / as soon as / before / until`.

**Distractors:** `will` after a time conjunction (the classic trap), `going to` where evidence is absent, the bare infinitive after `will`.

**Student errors:** `When I will finish…`; treating `will` and `going to` as interchangeable everywhere.

---

## Passive voice

**Test:** the passive across tenses (present simple, past simple, present perfect, modal passive); `by` + agent only when the agent matters. Do not try to test intransitive verbs here — a fill-the-blank item needs one correct form to key, and a verb with no passive has none.

**Distractors:** the active form, `be` omitted, the wrong `be` tense with the right participle, the base verb instead of the participle.

**Student errors:** `The letter was write`; `is been built`; forgetting the auxiliary in the present perfect passive (`has built` for `has been built`).

---

## Conditionals

**Test:** type 1 (real future), type 2 (unreal present / advice with `were`), type 3 (unreal past and regret); the ban on `will` and `would` inside the `if` clause; `If I were you`.

**Distractors:** `will` in the `if` clause, the wrong type's verb form, `would have` where `had` belongs, `was` where the conventional `were` is expected.

**Student errors:** `If I will see him…`; `If I would have known…`; mixing type 2 and type 3 across the sentence.

**Two traps when writing these items.** First, length: a type-3 answer (`would have collected`) is bigger than every plausible distractor (`would collect`, `had collected`, `will collect`), and in auxiliary-only question items the correct `Would` / `Could` / `Should` outruns `Will / Did / Has / Was`. Write a natural paper and you land near half the items keyed to the longest option. The honest fix is a distractor that is longer *and* a real mistake: where the stem already contains *have* (`______ she have applied earlier…?`), offer `Would have` — doubling *have* is a mistake students genuinely make. Pair `wouldn't build up` with `wouldn't have built up`, and length-match the chunk items (`would you have collected` against `you would have collected`). Second, counting: most conditional items carry a negation in one clause or the other, so far more of them classify as negative than you would guess. Check the balance against the rule in `formats.md` rather than by eye.

## Reported speech

**Test:** the backshift of tense, and of pronouns, time and place words (`now → then`, `tomorrow → the next day`, `here → there`); reported questions with no inversion and no `do/does/did`; reported commands with `to` + infinitive.

**Distractors:** the un-backshifted tense, question word order kept in the reported clause, `that` inserted into a reported question, `said` where `told` needs an object.

**Student errors:** `He asked me where did I live`; forgetting to backshift; `He said me`.

---

## Modals

**Test:** obligation and its absence (`must / have to / mustn't / don't have to`), ability past and present, permission and requests, deduction (`must be / can't be / might be`), and past modals (`should have + participle`).

**Distractors:** `mustn't` where `don't have to` is meant (opposite meanings — a rich source of good items), `to` after a modal, the past participle where the base verb belongs, `can` for a past context.

**Student errors:** `must to go`; treating `mustn't` and `don't have to` as the same; `could of`.

---

## Relative clauses

**Test:** `who / which / that / whose / where / when`; defining vs non-defining and the comma; when the pronoun can be omitted (object position, defining clause only); the verb agreeing with the noun the clause describes.

**Distractors:** `that` in a non-defining clause, `which` for a person, `who's` for `whose`, a doubled subject (`the man who he lives…`).

**Student errors:** the doubled subject; using `that` after a comma.

---

## Gerund vs infinitive

**Test:** verbs taking the gerund (enjoy, avoid, finish, suggest, mind, practise), verbs taking the infinitive (decide, hope, agree, promise, manage, refuse), the gerund after prepositions, and pairs that change meaning (`stop doing` vs `stop to do`, `remember doing` vs `remember to do`).

**Distractors:** the other form, the bare infinitive, `to` + gerund, the past participle.

**Student errors:** `I enjoy to read`; `He suggested to go`; `interested to learn`.

## Mixed revision papers

When the teacher asks for `mixed`, choose 5–8 topics and rotate so no two consecutive questions test the same one. A workable default spread at Bacaloria level:

present perfect vs past simple · passive voice · conditionals (types 1–3) · reported speech · modals · past perfect · gerund vs infinitive

Keep the roughly 20/15/15 balance of affirmative, negative and question sentences, shuffled through the paper with no part headings. Negative and question forms are harder in some topics than others — put the passive and reported-speech items in the back half of the paper, since those are where students lose most marks.
